Abstract—Online  learning  platforms  host  an  extensive  and 
rapidly  growing  collection  of  educational  content,  making  it 
increasingly difficult for learners to identify relevant courses. 
This paper presents CourseHub, a cross-platform course recom- 
mendation 
framework 
that 
leverages 
transformer-based 
semantic embeddings to align learner intent with appropriate 
educational resources. The proposed system utilizes the all-
MiniLM-L6- v2 model to generate 384-dimensional embeddings 
for  course  content,  enabling  efficient  semantic  similarity 
computation  using  cosine  similarity.  To  improve 
recommendation quality, a hybrid ranking strategy is employed 
that  combines  semantic  relevance  with  normalized  quality 
indicators  such  as  user  ratings  and  course  popularity. 
Experimental  evaluation  demonstrates  that  CourseHub 
significantly outperforms traditional keyword-based approaches, 
achieving a Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) of 0.883, Hit Rate@5 
of  92%,  and  NDCG@10  of  0.881.  Additionally,  the  system 
maintains low latency (under 250 ms at the 95th per- centile) and 
reduces the average course discovery-to-enrollment time by 43%. 
These 
results 
highlight 
the 
effectiveness 
of 
semantic 
representations in enhancing course discovery in modern educa- 
tional platforms.
Index Terms—Educational recommendation systems, semantic

I. INTRODUCTION
A. Motivation and Background
Over the  last  decade, digital  education has transformed 
learning in significant ways. What began as pilot distance 
learning  programs  has  evolved  into  a  complex  and  vast 
ecosystem of Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), with 
millions of users worldwide [1]. Platforms offering the 
greatest number of MOOCs include Coursera, Udemy, and 
edX, which  cumulatively  provide  over  200,000  distinct 
courses across almost every conceivable academic discipline.
This abundance, however, creates an unexpected challenge. 
When faced with thousands of apparently similar choices, 
learners  encounter  what  psychologists  refer  to  as  choice 
overload—a state of paralysis arising when decision-makers 
must evaluate an excessive number of alternatives [2]. Liter- 
ature indicates that prospective students spend approximately
2.5 hours evaluating courses before enrolling, and nearly two 
in five abandon their search without completing enrollment 
[3].
B. Core Challenges
Current course discovery systems in EdTech platforms face 
several fundamental limitations.
First, there is excessive reliance on keyword search. Most 
existing platforms employ exact or fuzzy string matching, 
which fails to capture the semantic intent of learner queries. 
Results for students interested in career transitions, prereq- 
uisite knowledge, or skill development are often ranked by 
keyword frequency rather than conceptual compatibility [4].
Second, learners must navigate disjointed platform ecosys- 
tems, 
performing 
multiple 
searches 
across 
different 
proprietary systems with varying interfaces and retrieval logic. 
This frag- mentation imposes significant cognitive overhead 
and hinders effective comparison of alternatives [5].
Third, existing systems lack contextual intelligence. Con- 
sider two students searching for machine learning courses: 
one is a beginner who needs foundational training, while 
the  other  is  an  experienced  practitioner  seeking  advanced 
specialization [5].
Fourth, while collaborative filtering performs well for pop- 
ular courses with extensive enrollment histories, it struggles 
with newly published content. This cold-start problem results 
in inadequate visibility for new courses that lack historical 
user interaction data [6].
C. Research Contribution
This 
paper 
presents 
CourseHub, 
a 
semantic 
recommendation  framework  designed  explicitly  to  address 
these shortcomings. The major contributions are as follows:
• A cross-platform aggregation system that integrates het- 
erogeneous course metadata into a standardized represen- 
tation schema.
• Implementation  of  state-of-the-art  transformer  embed- 
dings  to  extract  deep  semantic  relationships  between 
learner queries and course content.
• A multi-objective ranking algorithm that balances seman- 
tic relevance with empirical quality signals.
• Comprehensive empirical evaluation demonstrating sig- 
nificant performance improvements over traditional ap- 
proaches.
• Analysis of real-world deployment characteristics, 
includ- ing latency profiles and user experience metrics.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Recommendation System Fundamentals
Recommendation systems have progressed through distinct 
evolutionary 
phases. 
Early 
approaches 
centred 
on 
collaborative  filtering,  which  identifies  patterns  in  user 
behaviour to predict preferences [7]. While effective given 
sufficient  interaction  data,  collaborative  methods  face 
significant  challenges with new items or users—the  well-
documented cold-start prob- lem [7].
Content-based filtering emerged as an alternative paradigm, 
generating  recommendations  based  on  item  characteristics 
rather than behavioural patterns. Classic implementations em- 
ployed TF-IDF vectorisation of textual descriptions [8]. How- 
ever, while  TF-IDF excels at  exact  keyword matching, it 
cannot capture semantic relationships between conceptually 
similar but lexically distinct terms.
Hybrid recommendation systems that combine multiple ap- 
proaches have proven more effective. These systems are well 
suited for educational settings because they incorporate both 
content characteristics and auxiliary signals such as ratings, 
difficulty levels, instructor credentials, and user feedback [9].
B. Transformer Models and Semantic Embeddings
The  Transformer architecture revolutionised natural  lan- 
guage processing upon its introduction [10]. Unlike earlier 
recurrent neural network models, Transformers employ self- 
attention to dynamically weight word importance during se- 
mantic encoding, yielding substantial improvements in con- 
textual understanding.
BERT extended this by applying bidirectional pre-training, 
allowing the model to consider both preceding and following 
context simultaneously [11]. However, BERT’s original 
design  was  optimised  for  classification  tasks  rather  than 
similarity computation.
Sentence-BERT  addressed  this  limitation  by  modifying 
BERT’s training methodology using Siamese network struc- 
tures, producing embeddings where semantically related sen- 
tences occupy proximate positions in vector space [12]. This 
breakthrough enabled efficient semantic similarity computa- 
tion through cosine distance calculations.
Subsequent studies focused on model compression tech- 
niques that maintain performance while reducing computa- 
tional requirements. The MiniLM model family, including the 
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model deployed in CourseHub, achieves 
comparable  results  with  substantially  lower  computational 
overhead [13]. The education sector has increasingly demon- 
strated  the  utility  of  transformer  models  in  understanding 
learner intent, with applications spanning curriculum design, 
intelligent tutoring systems, and personalised learning path 
generation [14], [15].
C. EdTech and Course Recommendation Systems
Recommender systems for e-learning typically fall into two 
categories: those designed for learners with special require- 
ments and those intended for general use. Educational recom- 
mendations 
must 
account 
for 
prerequisites, 
learning 
objectives,
and  sequencing  that  may  influence  the  effectiveness  of  a 
recommended course of study [14].
Deep learning methods have recently been applied to 
educa-  tional 
recommendations. 
Recent 
studies 
have 
employed graph-  based  neural  architectures  for  modelling 
student-content rela- tionships, attention-based approaches for 
capturing temporal dynamics of learning, and multi-objective 
learning frameworks for jointly optimising engagement and 
learning outcomes [15].  Despite  these  advances,  most 
documented approaches rely on extensive usage history from 
a single platform, leaving a gap in systems capable of serving 
users with limited behavioural data across multiple platforms.
D. Semantic Search in Educational Contexts
Semantic  search  techniques  are  well  established  within 
educational technology. Prior work has applied semantic em- 
bedding techniques to scholarly article recommendation, ed- 
ucational resource alignment, and curriculum mapping [15]. 
Hybrid architectures that combine content features with aux- 
iliary signals such as ratings, difficulty levels, and instructor 
credentials are increasingly effective. Research on educational 
search  behaviour  has  found  that  successful  learners  often 
describe their goals in terms of career objectives or skills 
they wish to acquire, rather than using specific course titles. 
Semantic methods are well suited for such queries, as they 
can match diverse expressions of learner intent with relevant 
educational content.
III. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
CourseHub adopts a modular architecture organised into 
four principal layers, each handling specific responsibilities 
within  the  recommendation  pipeline.  Fig.  1  illustrates  the 
complete system design and data flow. The pipeline operates 
as follows: raw course data is ingested and normalised in 
Layer 1, semantically encoded into vector representations in 
Layer 2, ranked against a user query in Layer 3, and presented 
through the web interface in Layer 4. Each layer passes its 
output directly to the next, ensuring a clear and reproducible 
flow from raw data to final recommendation.
A. Data Ingestion and Normalization
The base layer handles data collection and standardisa- 
tion from multiple platforms. The current system integrates 
four major providers—Coursera, Udemy, edX, and Udacity— 
which collectively account for approximately 85% of the total 
MOOC market by enrolled student count.
Data Collection: Course metadata was retrieved program- 
matically via each platform’s publicly accessible API or struc- 
tured web interface in October 2024. For platforms without a 
formal API, structured HTTP requests with appropriate rate 
limiting were  used to collect  course  listings, descriptions, 
ratings, and enrollment figures.
Deduplication: Because the same course occasionally ap- 
pears  across  multiple  platforms  (e.g.,  a  university  course 
listed on both Coursera and edX), we applied a two-stage 
deduplication strategy. First, exact matches on course title

C. Similarity Score Distribution Analysis
Fig. 3 illustrates the distribution of cosine similarity scores 
between user queries and matched courses in the evaluation 
dataset.
Fig. 3. Similarity Score Distribution
The distribution exhibits several noteworthy characteristics. 
Quantitatively, 68.4% of all query–course pairs yield cosine 
similarity scores in the 0.0–0.2 range, confirming that the 
majority of the corpus is semantically distant from any given 
query. This mass of low-scoring pairs plays a critical filtering 
role: by applying a similarity threshold of 0.25, the candidate
pool is reduced by approximately 71% before the composite 
scoring  function  is  applied,  substantially  reducing  ranking 
computation. The right tail of the distribution (scores > 0.5) 
accounts for 8.2% of pairs and corresponds closely to the 
annotator-labelled “highly relevant” category; the precision 
of the embedding space in isolating this tail from the bulk 
of the  distribution is a key factor in CourseHub’s strong 
NDCG performance. The gradual decay of the tail (skewness
= 1.74) confirms that the embedding space encodes graded 
levels of semantic relatedness rather than producing a binary 
relevant/irrelevant separation.

G. User Experience Metrics
A small-scale user study was conducted to evaluate 
usability and practical effectiveness.
Participant Selection:  Twenty-four participants were re- 
cruited via a university mailing list. Eligibility criteria 
required that participants: (a) had used at least one MOOC 
platform  in  the  preceding  six  months,  (b)  were  currently 
seeking to learn a new skill or transition to a new career 
domain, and (c) represented a range of technical backgrounds 
(12 participants self-identified as technical/STEM users; 12 as 
non-technical users). Informed consent was obtained from all 
participants prior to the study.
Experimental Protocol:  Each participant completed two 
course-search tasks using CourseHub and two equivalent tasks 
using  a  native  platform  search  interface  (counterbalanced 
across participants to control for order effects). Tasks were 
designed to represent realistic learning goals, such as “find a 
course to help me transition into data analytics” and “find 
a beginner course on graphic design.” Each session lasted 
approximately 30 minutes.
Evaluation Questionnaire: After completing all tasks, par- 
ticipants responded to a structured questionnaire comprising 
seven  items  measured  on  a  five-point  Likert  scale.  Items 
assessed: result relevance, ease of query formulation, interface
intuitiveness, cross-platform comparison utility, overall satis- 
faction, time efficiency, and likelihood of re-use. Two open- 
ended questions invited participants to describe the most and 
least useful aspects of each system.
Findings: 87.5% of participants rated CourseHub as more 
effective than native platform search. The mean time from 
first search to course enrollment decreased to 24 minutes—a 
43% reduction compared to the 42-minute baseline. The 
cross- platform comparison feature was rated highly useful by 
79.2% of participants, and 91.7% found the natural language 
interface intuitive.
VI. LIMITATIONS
Although CourseHub achieves strong results, several limi- 
tations remain. The system does not incorporate personalised 
user modelling, which may reduce adaptability to individual 
learner preferences. The dataset, while large, is a sample of 
available course offerings and may not fully represent the 
diversity of all MOOC platforms. Future work will address 
these limitations by incorporating user interaction data and 
expanding platform coverage.
VII. CONCLUSION
This paper introduced CourseHub, a semantic course 
recom- mendation framework that addresses key limitations of 
existing  educational  search  systems.  By  combining 
transformer-based embeddings with a multi-objective ranking 
algorithm, Course- Hub achieves substantial improvements 
over both classical and modern neural retrieval baselines, as 
validated by statis- tically significant results across multiple 
evaluation metrics. CourseHub attained an MRR of 0.883, Hit 
Rate@5 of 92%, and NDCG@10 of 0.881. User study results 
confirm practical utility, with participants reporting a 43% 
reduction in search- to-enrollment time and high satisfaction 
scores.  Future  work  will  investigate  personalised  ranking 
incorporating  individual  learner  profiles,  integration  of 
additional quality signals such as course completion rates and 
skill outcomes, and multilin- gual support for global course 
discovery.
