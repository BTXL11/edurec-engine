a b s t r a c t
The course recommendation system is a core application of recommendation technology in the educational ﬁeld. 
Its signiﬁcance lies in accurately matching users’ interests and needs while providing valuable feedback to in-
structors, thereby fostering continuous improvement in teaching quality. Various techniques have been proposed 
for this purpose, with Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrating signiﬁcant potential in course recommen-
dation tasks. However, the issue of data sparsity remains a critical bottleneck that limits the accuracy of the 
recommendation. In this study, we propose a Dual Relationship Graph (DRG) framework that addresses data 
sparsity by modeling both course-course and user-course relationships through a dual-graph structure. Specif-
ically, DRG constructs two relational graphs: a course-based graph built using LLM-based semantic reasoning, 
collaborative ﬁltering, clustering, and association rule mining; and a user-based graph constructed via collab-
orative ﬁltering and LLM-based preference inference. These graphs are integrated into a uniﬁed recommenda-
tion pipeline through joint graph learning and collaborative reasoning. The enhanced interaction graphs signif-
icantly alleviated sparsity, increasing link coverage by 37.88 % and 12.67 % on the two datasets, respectively. 
Notably, DRG is designed as a plug-and-play module, compatible with both traditional models and LLM-based 
recommendation systems. Experimental results show that our DRG excels in task ranking across two benchmark 
datasets, signiﬁcantly enhancing traditional recommendation models and LLM-based methods. Moreover, DRG’s 
dual relationship graph consistently outperforms single relationship approaches, underscoring the importance 
of multi-perspective integration in course recommendation systems. By unifying dual-perspective graph model-
ing with LLM-driven semantic understanding, DRG provides a scalable and eﬀective solution for personalized 
course recommendation in sparse educational environments. The code and datasets will be made available at 
https://github.com/WHCK1102/DRG.
1.  Introduction
With the rapid expansion of online education, numerous MOOC 
(Massive Open Online Course) platforms now oﬀer an overwhelming 
number of course options to a highly diverse user base (Khalid et al., 
2022). Course recommendation systems, as a specialized application of 
recommendation technology in education, focus on helping users pre-
cisely match courses that best align with their interests and learning 
needs (Jing & Tang, 2017). However, in practice, these systems often 
suﬀer from sparse user-course interaction data, a challenge exacerbated 
by the surge in course options, the diversity of learners, and the fact 
that most users enroll in only a few courses, resulting in limited behav-
ioral signals for modeling preferences (Algarni & Sheldon, 2023). This 
sparsity signiﬁcantly degrades the accuracy of the recommendation, es-
pecially for new or less active users, and limits the system’s ability to

2.  Related work
With the rapid expansion of course resources on large-scale online 
learning platforms, users are increasingly faced with the problem of in-
formation overload (Aher & Lobo, 2013). Course recommendation sys-
tems have gained signiﬁcant attention as an eﬀective solution, aiming to 
capture the unique characteristics of learners and learning needs to pro-
vide personalized course suggestions (Symeonidis & Malakoudis, 2019). 
In this section, we brieﬂy review the literature most relevant to our 
work, focusing on traditional course recommendation approaches and 
the emerging application of LLMs in educational recommendation sys-
tems.
2.1.  Traditional methods for course recommendation

niques were explored to model user preferences and course relation-
ships. These included CF-based methods (Ray & Sharma, 2011; Symeoni-
dis & Malakoudis, 2019), prerequisite-based course modeling (Chanaa 
& El Faddouli, 2024; Parameswaran et al., 2011), evaluation techniques

granularity of available features.
2.2.  Application of LLMs in education
Recently, LLMs have attracted increasing attention in the educa-
tion domain, demonstrating potential across diverse applications such 
as providing student feedback and assistance (Bauer et al., 2023; Dai 
et al., 2023), generating course concepts (Ehara, 2023), oﬀering tutoring 
support (Pardos & Bhandari, 2023), evaluating open-ended questions 
(Botelho et al., 2023), providing programming error feedback (Phung 
et al., 2023), and supporting AI training (Chang et al., 2022; Markel 
et al., 2023). In the area of course recommendation, several studies have 
begun exploring the use of LLMs to address challenges like data spar-
sity and personalization (Abu-Rasheed et al., 2024a; Lyu et al., 2023; 
Ma et al., 2025). For example, Van Deventer et al. (2024) proposed an 
LLM-based course recommendation system using retrieval augmented 
generation on course descriptions. In this context, Rao and Lin (2024) 
introduced a framework that uses LLMs to improve recommendation ac-
curacy, mitigate cold-start problems, and support personalized learning. 
Similarly, Khan et al. (2024) applied LLMs in Educational Data Min-
ing to enhance recommendation performance, and Abu-Rasheed et al. 
(2024b) integrated LLMs with knowledge graphs to support problem-
based learning path generation. Building on these eﬀorts, this paper ex-
plores the integration of LLMs with complementary techniques such as 
course planning, clustering, and collaborative ﬁltering to uncover la-
tent course relationships. The objective is to improve recommendation 
accuracy, alleviate the eﬀects of interaction sparsity, and enhance the 
pedagogical relevance of the recommended courses.
3.  Method
This section presents the Dual Relational Graph (DRG) framework 
for course recommendation. As shown in Fig. 2, the overall framework 
consists of three key stages: (1) preprocessing of user-course interaction 
data, (2) construction of two complementary relationship graphs, and 
(3) recommendation generation through joint reasoning. Speciﬁcally, 
we ﬁrst leverage historical interaction data to construct two comple-
mentary graphs: one course-based and one user-based. The course-based 
graph integrates multiple sources-LLM reasoning, collaborative ﬁltering, 
clustering, and association rule mining-to capture inter-course relation-
ships. The user-based graph combines LLM reasoning with collaborative 
ﬁltering to reveal user preferences. These graphs provide structural and 
semantic representations that serve as the foundation for personalized 
recommendation.
To further clarify the internal workﬂow of the DRG framework, Fig. 3 
presents the architectural pipeline outlining the end-to-end data process-
ing and recommendation ﬂow. Starting from a learner’s historical course 
records, DRG constructs course-based and user-based relationship en-
hancement through a combination of techniques. These enhanced rela-
tionships form a dual relational graph, which is used to generate can-
didate course recommendations. A tailored prompting mechanism then 
transforms these candidates into structured inputs for the LLM-based 
model, which generates recommendation results. The output is sub-
jected to expert judgment (system-based reliability checks). If the rec-
ommendation passes the reliability threshold, it is delivered to the user; 
otherwise, the system triggers a retry mechanism to re-evaluate candi-
date relationships and regenerate recommendations. Notably, DRG is 
designed as a plug-and-play framework, making it applicable to a wide 
range of traditional recommendation systems as well as LLM-based sys-
tems.

tory interaction data ingestion, multi-source relationship mining, dual

To thoroughly analyze the intrinsic connections among courses and 
eﬀectively address the challenges posed by data sparsity, we carefully 
constructed a multi-dimensional, integrative course relationship en-

enable a detailed and nuanced capture of the complex interrelationships

3.2.4.  Association rules-based relationship mining
To further explore potential relationships among courses, we em-
ployed a methodology combining course plans with association rule 
mining. This approach aims to uncover richer logical sequences and pre-
requisite relationships, thereby enhancing the authenticity of our course 
relationship graph. Speciﬁcally, ﬁrst, we normalized course informa-
tion using TF-IDF and keyword matching to align it with our MOOC 
dataset. We extracted prerequisite relationships through both explicit 
course sequencing in academic plans and implicit patterns revealed by 
association rule analysis. Finally, these relationships underwent rigor-
ous validation by domain experts to ensure their accuracy. For exam-
ple, as illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the user must master the concepts of 
“Discrete Mathematics” and “Probability Theory” before studying “Data 
Structures”. This reﬂects the complex dependencies and progression 
among courses, highlighting that learning is a systematic and continuous
process.
Next, we utilized the Apriori association rule (Agrawal & Srikant, 
1994) mining to further investigate the relationships among courses. 
Firstly, we transformed student’ historical course records into a trans-
actional format suitable for association rule mining. Secondly, we em-
ployed the Apriori algorithm to mine association rules based on two 
key indicators: support and conﬁdence, where support is the frequency 
of co-occurrence of courses in enrollment records, and conﬁdence is 
the possibility that students who took course 𝑐1 also take course 𝑐2. 
These metrics were chosen to identify meaningful and statistically sig-
niﬁcant course relationships. Subsequently, the mined rules were ﬁl-
tered and validated against the course plan (e.g., prerequisites, follow-
up courses) to ensure logical consistency. Rules that aligned with 
the course plan were retained. Through the above steps, the associa-
tion rules allowed us to uncover hidden course relationships and en-
hance the course plan with data-driven insights. These combinations 
reveal patterns of co-occurrence, providing robust evidence for course
recommendations.
This comprehensive approach enabled us to uncover rich and com-
plex interrelations among courses, culminating in the course relation-
ship graph 𝐺𝐶= (𝐶1, 𝐸1). This graph serves as a robust foundation for 
delivering highly personalized and accurate course recommendations.

4.  Experiments
In this section, we will ﬁrst describe the dataset, the basic ex-
perimental settings, and some classic models and large-scale model
baselines. Then, we will validate the speciﬁc eﬀects of our proposed 
DRG framework on two educational datasets, MOOCCube and MOOC-
Data. Additionally, we will analyze the generalize ability of DRG to an-
swer four research questions:
• RQ1 Does the proposed DRG framework improve existing state-of-
the-art recommenders across various experimental environments?
• RQ2 What is the impact of diﬀerent enhanced DRG frameworks on

• RQ3 Does diﬀerent parameter settings aﬀect our DRG framework?

4.1.  Dataset
We selected two publicly available real-world datasets, MOOCCube 
and MOOCData, to evaluate our approach. Table 1 summarizes the rel-
evant statistics of these datasets.
MOOCCube is a large-scale, open-source dataset designed to support 
research in massive open online education2. It contains course metadata, 
historical learning records, and social information reﬂecting the learn-
ing behaviors of users. For this study, we utilized the MOOCQA sub-
set of the repository, which focuses on user participation data relevant 
to course recommendation tasks (Yu et al., 2020). The ﬁnal processed 
dataset includes 706 courses, 4723 users, and 24,933 user-course inter-
action records, which were converted into the interaction data format 
required by recommendation systems.
MOOCData is collected from XuetangX3, a leading MOOC platform 
in China (Zhang et al., 2019). To ensure the representativeness and prac-
tical utility of this dataset, we applied the following preprocessing steps: 
(1) Merged identical course content oﬀered in diﬀerent years into a sin-
gle course to eliminate the inﬂuence of temporal factors on data consis-
tency. (2) Selected users who enrolled in at least ten courses, ensuring 
that they had suﬃcient learning sequences and diverse interests. (3) Re-
tained only courses that had been enrolled by at least ten users, ensuring 
suﬃcient engagement and analyzability. The resulting dataset consists 
of 667 courses, 8216 users, and 129,643 interaction records.
To evaluate the proposed DRG framework, both datasets were split 
into training, validation, and test sets in a 7:2:1 ratio. This split ensures a 
robust evaluation by allocating 70 % of the data for training the model, 
20 % for validation (to tune hyperparameters and prevent overﬁtting), 
and 10 % for testing (to assess the model’s generalization performance 
on unseen data).

4.2.  Experimental settings
4.2.1.  Evaluation metrics
To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the DRG frame-
work, following the same setting as previous baselines (He et al., 2020; 
Li et al., 2023), we employed three widely adopted ranking-based met-
rics: Precision@K (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2019), Recall@K (Kynkään-
niemi et al., 2019), and NDCG@K (Yining et al., 2013). Speciﬁcally, 
Precision@K measures the alignment between the top-K recommended 
courses and the courses the user is actually interested in. Recall@K eval-
uates the proportion of courses the user is genuinely interested in that 
were correctly recommended within the top-K positions. NDCG@K fur-
ther considers the ranking quality within the recommendation list, as-
signing higher importance to correct recommendations appearing ear-
lier in the list. Unless otherwise speciﬁed, Precision@K, Recall@K, and 
NDCG@K are abbreviated as P@K, R@K and N@K, respectively. For 
a fair comparison, we follow most baseline methods (e.g., LightGCN, 
NGCF, and EHGCN ) and set the evaluation metrics P@K, R@K and 
N@K to 20, respectively.
Note that in all experimental evaluations involving LLM-based and 
traditional methods, we employed Recall, NDCG, and Precision metrics 
to evaluate traditional course recommendation models, while only Pre-
cision was used to evaluate the LLM-based approach. This diﬀerence 
in evaluation metrics is due to the distinct characteristics and evalua-
tion goals of the LLM-based model. Speciﬁcally, the LLM is a generative 
model that outputs a list of candidate courses rather than a directly 
ranked recommendation list. Since the candidate courses generated by 
the LLM require further processing (e.g., ranking or ﬁltering), directly 
evaluating Recall and NDCG may not be appropriate.
4.2.2.  Implementation details
All experiments were implemented using PyTorch on a 12 GB Nvidia 
RTX 4070 GPU. We tuned the parameters of traditional models, includ-
ing the learning rate and the number of hidden layers, within the ranges 
of {0.00001, 0.001, …, 0.1} and {1, 2, …, 5}, respectively. To address 
the issue of course data sparsity, we employed various machine learn-
ing techniques alongside OpenAI’s ChatGPT model (speciﬁcally, gpt-3.5-
turbo) to generate courses that may engage learners. For the large lan-
guage model, we tuned the temperature parameter over the set {0.1, 0.3, 
..., 0.9} to control the randomness of the generated content. Addition-
ally, we assessed the impact of diﬀerent learning rates {0.00001, 0.001, 
..., 0.1} on the ﬁne-tuning performance of LLM-based components. To 
ensure a fair comparison, both our approach and the baselines used a 
ﬁxed embedding size of 64.
4.2.3.  Baselines
To comprehensively compare and validate the performance of the 
DRG framework, we selected two groups of baseline methods: deep 
learning-based approaches and large language model-based approaches. 
The speciﬁc baselines are as follows:
• LightGCN (He et al., 2020). It is a lightweight GCN model that sim-
pliﬁes the architecture by retaining only the neighbor aggregation 
operation, while removing feature transformation and non-linear ac-
tivation. This streamlined structure enables eﬃcient performance in 
recommendation tasks.
• EHGCN (Li et al., 2023). It is a hybrid GCN model that combines 
the strengths of GCN and HyperGCN to capture both pairwise and 
higher-order complex interactions between users and items. This 
approach generates richer user and item representations, enabling 
more accurate predictions of user preferences.
• NGCF (Wang et al., 2019). It is a GNN-based CF recommendation 
model. By performing embedding propagation on the user-item in-
teraction graph, and explicitly encodes collaborative signals in the 
form of higher-order connections, thereby reﬁning the embeddings 
of users and items and enhancing the performance of the recommen-
dation system.

4.3.  Recommended performance comparison (RQ1)
To demonstrate the eﬀectiveness of DRG in improving recommen-
dation performance, we integrate it into four state-of-the-art traditional 
GNN-based models and four LLM-based models. Tables 2 and 3 present 
the original performance of all models and their enhanced performance 
on two publicly available datasets, MOOCCube and MOOCData.
Comparing the evaluation results of the two tables, several inter-
esting observations are found: (1) It is evident that, following the en-
hancement with the DRG framework, all models exhibited signiﬁcant 
improvements over their original versions across all evaluation metrics. 
This provides compelling evidence for the reasonableness and eﬀective-
ness of DRG. We attribute these enhancements to two main factors: i) 
DRG enables precise course recommendations supported by LLMs, and 
ii) the construction of multiple relationship graphs maximally exploits 
the interconnected information among courses, thereby eﬀectively en-
hancing the ﬁnal recommendation outcomes. (2) Overall, the percent-
age improvement is higher for traditional models compared to LLM-
based models, indirectly indicating that the integration of DRG with 
traditional models enhances the accuracy of course recommendations. 
(3) In terms of Precision evaluation, the traditional model LightGCN and 
the LLM-based model ChatGLM-6b demonstrate the best performance 
both before and after enhancement. For the Recall and NDCG evalua-

on the MOOCCube dataset. This diﬀerence may stem from ChatGLM-
6b’s superior generalization capability, making it adaptable to diverse 
application scenarios and user needs. In contrast, the GenRec model may 
focus more on speciﬁc domains or datasets, and fail to eﬀectively lever-
age the enhanced relational data.

4.5.  Impact of the parameter settings in the DRG framework (RQ3)
To improve the accuracy of the course recommendation system inte-
grating the DRG framework, we conducted a series of parameter tuning 
experiments to determine the optimal model conﬁguration. For the tra-
ditional model, we focused on adjusting the learning rate and hidden 
layer parameters, while for the LLM-based model, we prioritized tuning 
the learning rate and temperature coeﬃcient.
Here, we present LightGCN and ChatGLM-6b as representative mod-
els, with the results summarized in Fig. 6, leading to three key obser-
vations: (1) The learning rate is a critical parameter in model training. 
For the traditional model, the best performance was observed at a learn-
ing rate of 1 × 10−3. However, for the LLM-based model, adjusting the 
learning rate to 1 × 10−4 resulted in optimal performance. (2) The tra-
ditional model shows relative insensitivity to the conﬁguration of the 
hidden layer, especially when the hidden layer is set to 2 layers, achiev-
ing the best performance. (3) The LLM-based method shows low sen-
sitivity to the temperature coeﬃcient. No matter how the temperature 
coeﬃcient changes, the prediction index maintains a small ﬂuctuation 
range, which shows that the eﬀect of this parameter on the model per-
formance is relatively limited. These ﬁndings highlight the importance 
of tailored parameter tuning for both traditional and LLM-based models, 
demonstrating how careful optimization can signiﬁcantly enhance the 
performance of course recommendation systems.

tinct phases and patterns. Speciﬁcally, user 𝑢96 is currently focused 
on studying highly applied courses. In this context, the course “Cloud
Computing and Software Engineering” recommended by ChatGLM-6b 
precisely aligns with the users’ actual course choice, while the course 
“Software Engineering” recommended by EHGCN is reasonably aligned 
in direction. In contrast, LightGCN’s recommendation of “Introduction 
to Computer Graphics” appears somewhat misaligned, lacking clear rel-
evance to the users’ recent course interactions.
Overall, by integrating the DRG framework into the model, it can 
more eﬀectively capture the relationships among courses and analyze 
the underlying logic in users’ historical course interactions, enabling 
more accurate course recommendations.

5.  Conclusions
This paper addresses the challenge of course recommendation in 
online education by introducing an innovative Dual Relational Graph 
(DRG) framework. This framework leverages the advanced reasoning 
capabilities and extensive knowledge base of LLMs while seamlessly
integrating traditional methods such as collaborative ﬁltering, cluster-
ing, and lesson planning. The DRG framework eﬀectively mitigates 
the issue of data sparsity and uncovers intricate course relationships. 
Experimental results demonstrate that, compared to the original ver-
sion, integrating the DRG framework with the backbone recommenders 
leads to signiﬁcant performance improvements on two public bench-
mark datasets.
6.  Limitations
Nevertheless, the study has some limitations. First, while the DRG 
framework reduces the impact of data sparsity, there remains room for 
improvement in handling extremely sparse data scenarios. Second, the 
training and inference processes of LLMs require signiﬁcant computa-
tional resources, making model optimization to reduce costs a key di-
rection for future research. Additionally, this study has only been vali-
dated on two public datasets, and further evaluation and optimization 
are needed in more diverse ﬁelds and real-world application settings. 
Looking ahead, we will continue to deepen our research on this frame-
work, aiming to further contribute to the advancement of online educa-
tion.
